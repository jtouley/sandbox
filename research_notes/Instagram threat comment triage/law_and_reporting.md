## 1. True threats doctrine: when is a threatening comment unprotected?

### Takeaway
A "true threat" is a serious expression that the speaker means to commit unlawful violence against a particular person or group. Since *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023), the prosecution must also show the speaker was at least reckless: he was aware others could see the statement as threatening and posted it anyway. Hyperbole, jokes, and political exaggeration are protected. A specific comment aimed at an identifiable creator ("I'll run you over and kill you") is much more likely to be a true threat than a general rant about "cyclists."

### Cited Findings
- *Counterman v. Colorado*, 600 U.S. 66 (2023), Kagan J. for the majority. The First Amendment requires proof that the defendant had "some subjective understanding of the threatening character of his statements," and recklessness is enough. Recklessness here means the speaker "consciously disregard[ed] a substantial risk that his communications would be viewed as threatening violence." Colorado's purely objective "reasonable person" standard was held insufficient, and the conviction was vacated. — [Cornell LII, Counterman v. Colorado](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-138)
- *Counterman* defines true threats as "serious expression[s]" conveying that a speaker means to "commit an act of unlawful violence." Whether a statement is a threat depends on what it conveys to the recipient. The category excludes "jests" and "hyperbole" (citing *Watts*). The Court chose recklessness over purpose or knowledge partly because its defamation precedent uses a recklessness standard. — [Cornell LII, Counterman](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-138)
- Justice Sotomayor concurred in part, joined in part by Gorsuch, and would have required intent as the general rule. Justices Barrett and Thomas dissented in favor of an objective standard. So the standard was contested, but recklessness is now the constitutional floor. — [Cornell LII, Counterman](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-138)
- *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723 (2015). Elonis posted violent Facebook "rap lyrics" about his wife. The Court reversed his conviction under 18 U.S.C. 875(c) as a matter of statutory interpretation: negligence (what a reasonable person would think) is not enough for 875(c). The Court left open whether recklessness suffices and did not reach the First Amendment question. — [Columbia Global Freedom of Expression](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/cases/elonis-v-united-states/); [EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2015/06/threat-statute-requires-jury-consider-what-speaker-thinking); [Justia opinion](https://ussupremecourtopinions.justia.com/2015/06/01/elonis-v-united-states)
- *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969). An 18-year-old at a 1966 anti-draft rally said that if made to carry a rifle, "the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J." The Court held this was "political hyperbole," not a true threat. Context mattered: the statement was conditional, the crowd laughed, and it was made at a political rally. [not fetched this session] — [Cornell LII, Watts](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/394/705)
- *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003). True threats encompass "statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." The speaker need not actually intend to carry out the threat. Intimidation is a type of true threat. [not fetched this session] — [Cornell LII, Virginia v. Black](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/01-1107.ZO.html). *Counterman* says *Black* did not settle the mental-state question. — [Cornell LII, Counterman](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-138)
- Conditional threats in California law: summaries of PC 422 say conditional or "empty" threats generally do not qualify. However, the statute and case law treat a threat as criminal if, "on its face and under the circumstances," it is "so unequivocal, unconditional, immediate, and specific" that it conveys a gravity of purpose and an immediate prospect of execution. — [ShouseLaw PC 422 summary (defense firm)](https://www.shouselaw.com/ca/defense/penal-code/422/); statute at [california.public.law PC 422](https://california.public.law/codes/penal_code_section_422)

### Inferences
- Applied to the motorist example:
  - **"I'll run you over and kill you" aimed at a named creator** is threat language, not opinion. It names a method (vehicle) and a target, and the commenter knows where the creator rides because of the content. This is the kind of comment that plausibly crosses the line. Under *Counterman*, typing that directly to someone is strong circumstantial evidence of recklessness.
  - **"Someone should run these cyclists over" or "all cyclists deserve to be hit"** is generic, aimed at a class, and has no identifiable target. *Black* does allow threats against a "group of individuals," but in practice prosecutors rarely charge generic group rants unless other context exists, such as a specific event, location, or repeated targeting.
  - **Emojis, "lol," and replies that read as jokes** cut toward hyperbole (*Watts*).
- Factors that raise seriousness:
  - specificity of time, place, or method
  - knowledge of the victim's routes, home, car, or schedule
  - repetition, or a follow-up via DM
  - the commenter's real-name account and apparent proximity (same city)
  - references to having done it before
  - prior real-world incidents
- Factors that lower seriousness:
  - conditional phrasing ("if I ever see you...")
  - obvious exaggeration
  - generic class targets
  - accounts in other countries, which mostly removes US prosecution as a practical option

### Gaps
- No post-*Counterman* appellate decision applying it specifically to a social-media comment on a creator's video was located in this session.
- No authoritative source on how "group" threats (e.g., "all cyclists") have fared in prosecutions.

## 2. Federal statutes: 18 U.S.C. 875(c) and 2261A, and prosecution thresholds

### Takeaway
18 U.S.C. 875(c) makes it a felony, punishable by up to 5 years, to transmit an interstate communication containing a threat to injure. An internet post generally satisfies the interstate element. 18 U.S.C. 2261A(2), cyberstalking, requires a *course of conduct* (more than one act). Federal prosecutors bring these cases often, but the published examples overwhelmingly involve threats against public officials, schools, or groups targeted for bias, or sustained campaigns. A single comment to a private person is more often left to local or state authorities.

### Cited Findings
- 875(c) text: "Whoever transmits in interstate or foreign commerce any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another, shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more than five years, or both." — [Cornell LII, 18 U.S.C. 875](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/875)
- 2261A(2) applies to anyone who, "with the intent to kill, injure, harass, intimidate, or place under surveillance," uses "the mail, any interactive computer service or electronic communication service" or another interstate facility to engage in a course of conduct. That conduct must either (A) place the person in reasonable fear of death or serious bodily injury, or (B) cause, attempt to cause, or reasonably be expected to cause substantial emotional distress. Penalties are as provided in 18 U.S.C. 2261(b). — [Cornell LII, 18 U.S.C. 2261A](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2261A)
- Recent DOJ examples of social-media threat prosecutions:
  - Trent Schneider (N.D. Ill.) was sentenced to more than three years. On Oct. 21, 2025, he posted an Instagram video saying judges, doctors, lawyers, and police should be killed, and he was convicted of making a threat in interstate commerce. — [DOJ N.D. Ill.](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ndil/pr/suburban-chicago-man-sentenced-more-three-years-federal-prison-threatening-kill)
  - A W.D. Mich. defendant posted threatening comments on YouTube videos and was sentenced in 2026. — [DOJ W.D. Mich. 2026 Plyler](https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdmi/pr/2026_0915_Plyler_Sentencing_PR)
  - A Maine man posted YouTube comments threatening to hunt down and kill immigrants and was sentenced to time served in Oct. 2025. — [DOJ D. Me.](https://www.justice.gov/usao-me/pr/waterville-man-sentenced-making-interstate-threats)
  - A Maryland online-threats sentencing. — [DOJ D. Md.](https://www.justice.gov/usao-md/pr/maryland-man-sentenced-federal-prison-making-online-threats)
  - A Cedar Rapids man was sentenced to two years for threatening to kill a victim. — [DOJ N.D. Iowa](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ndia/pr/cedar-rapids-man-who-threatened-kill-victim-sentenced-two-years-federal-prison)
  - (Search-result headlines; the details of the last three were not fully read.)
- *Elonis* requires more than negligence for 875(c), and *Counterman* sets recklessness as the constitutional minimum. — [Columbia GFoE](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/cases/elonis-v-united-states/); [Cornell LII, Counterman](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-138)

### Inferences
- The search returned DOJ releases mostly about threats to officials, mass-violence threats, or bias-motivated threats, which suggests federal priorities lean that way. A one-off "I'll run you over" comment to a cyclist influencer is more likely to be handled, if at all, under state law (PC 422 / 653m) by the local agency where the victim lives.
- Federal interest rises when:
  - the threat crosses state lines and local police can't act
  - there's a sustained campaign (2261A)
  - there's a bias or terrorism angle
  - the account sends many threats to many people

### Gaps
- The DOJ Justice Manual / Criminal Resource Manual sections on 875 and 2261A could not be retrieved (404). No published DOJ numerical threshold for declining threat cases exists or was found.
- No federal prosecution involving threats against a cyclist or cycling content creator was found.

## 3. California (and other state) law; civil remedies

### Takeaway
In California, the main criminal statutes are:
- **PC 422 (criminal threats).** A wobbler that requires a threat of death or great bodily injury that causes the victim "sustained fear." A felony conviction is a strike.
- **PC 653m.** Covers obscene or threatening electronic communications made with intent to annoy or harass.
- **PC 646.9 (stalking).** Covers a credible threat plus a course of conduct.

The main civil tool is a **civil harassment restraining order (CCP 527.6)**. It requires clear and convincing evidence and generally more than one act, unless there is violence or a credible threat of violence.

### Cited Findings
- PC 422 elements, per the statute and CALCRIM 1300:
  - a willful threat to commit a crime that would result in death or great bodily injury
  - made with specific intent that it be taken as a threat (even if there's no intent to carry it out)
  - made verbally, in writing, or "by means of an electronic communication device"
  - "unequivocal, unconditional, immediate, and specific" under the circumstances
  - causing the person to be "reasonably" in "sustained fear" for their own or their immediate family's safety
  - — [california.public.law PC 422](https://california.public.law/codes/penal_code_section_422); [Justia 2024 CA Code PC 422](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-pen/part-1/title-11-5/section-422)
- "Sustained fear" means a period "that extends beyond what is momentary, fleeting, or transitory." PC 422 is a wobbler. As a felony it is a "serious felony" under PC 1192.7(c)(38) and a strike. — [ShouseLaw (defense firm summary)](https://www.shouselaw.com/ca/defense/penal-code/422/); see *People v. Toledo* (Cal. 2001) on attempted criminal threats [SCOCAL](https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/people-v-toledo-32222)
- The required fear has three components: actual, reasonable, and sustained. If the victim was not actually scared, there is no PC 422 violation. — [ShouseLaw](https://www.shouselaw.com/ca/defense/penal-code/422/)
- CCP 527.6 defines harassment as "unlawful violence, a credible threat of violence, or a knowing and willful course of conduct" directed at a specific person. The conduct must seriously alarm, annoy, or harass, and must serve no legitimate purpose. A course of conduct is more than one act, and constitutionally protected activity is excluded. The standard is clear and convincing evidence (527.6(i)). — [california.public.law CCP 527.6](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_civ_proc_code_section_527.6); [WomensLaw CA](https://www.womenslaw.org/laws/ca/statutes/5276-harassment-temporary-restraining-order-and-order-after-hearing-procedure)
- Court self-help packets say "a one-time, non-violent incident is not sufficient." A petitioner need not live in California to file. — [El Dorado Superior Court CH packet](https://www.eldorado.courts.ca.gov/system/files/forms-and-filings/request-civil-harassment-restraining-orders-packet.pdf); [Ventura Superior Court](https://ventura.courts.ca.gov/self-help/civil-harassment-restraining-orders)
- *Hansen v. Volkov* (Cal. Ct. App. 2023) held it was error to count constitutionally protected emails as part of a harassing course of conduct. Online speech must be unprotected, such as a true threat, to count. — [Justia, Hansen v. Volkov](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2023/b311524.html)
- PC 653m: a misdemeanor for making contact by "electronic communication device" with intent to annoy, using obscene language or a threat to inflict injury to the person or property of the recipient or a family member. [statute text not fetched this session — verify] — [california.public.law PC 653m](https://california.public.law/codes/penal_code_section_653m)

### Inferences
- A creator who receives one "I'll run you over" comment from a stranger may be able to show a "credible threat of violence," which can qualify for a restraining order even without a course of conduct, if the threat is credible. But the petitioner must identify and serve the respondent, which is often impossible for pseudonymous accounts. A restraining order is realistic mainly when the commenter is identifiable, local, and repeat.
- PC 422's "sustained fear" requirement means a victim who honestly shrugs a comment off may not have a 422 case. Police reports should accurately describe the victim's actual reaction, such as changing routes or not posting live locations.
- Civil damages suits (assault does not fit online speech well; intentional infliction of emotional distress or civil stalking under Civ. Code 1708.7 may) are possible, but they are expensive and rarely worthwhile against judgment-proof individuals. [no source fetched; inference]

### Gaps
- Colorado (C.R.S. 18-9-111 harassment / 18-3-602 stalking, the statute at issue in *Counterman*) and New York (Penal Law 240.30, aggravated harassment 2nd degree) texts were not retrieved this session. The report writer should present them only generically or verify them first. Note that *Counterman* arose under Colorado's stalking statute, 18-3-602(1)(c). [Cornell LII, Counterman](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-138)
- Current post-*Counterman* CALCRIM 1300 mental-state wording was not verified.

## 4. Evidence preservation

### Takeaway
The victim should immediately capture full-context screenshots or screen recordings showing the comment, the commenter's handle and profile URL, the post URL, and the date and time. Export the data with a hash or archive copy, log what was captured and when, and do not engage with the commenter. Only law enforcement can force Meta to preserve data, using an 18 U.S.C. 2703(f) request. Meta holds data for 90 days on such a request. Absent a preservation request, deleted content may be lost.

### Cited Findings
- Meta "will take steps to preserve account records in connection with official criminal investigations for 90 days" pending formal legal process. Meta does "not retain data for law enforcement purposes unless we receive a valid preservation request" before the user deletes it. — [Meta Information for Law Enforcement Authorities](https://www.meta.com/safety/communities/law/guidelines/)
- Meta "will not review or respond to requests submitted by non-law enforcement officials." Private parties, including civil litigants, are directed to the Help Center. Users can use "Download Your Information," but "Users do not have access to historical IP information without legal process." — [Meta LE guidelines](https://www.meta.com/safety/communities/law/guidelines/)
- What police need to get from Meta (US):
  - **Basic subscriber records** (name, length of service, email, recent login IPs): a subpoena.
  - **Other non-content records:** a 2703(d) court order.
  - **Stored content** (posts, messages, photos, location): a search warrant.
  - End-to-end-encrypted Messenger content is no longer available unencrypted.
  - — [Meta LE guidelines](https://www.meta.com/safety/communities/law/guidelines/)
- The FBI's Threat Intimidation Guide says to "Write the exact wording of the threat." For electronic threats, it advises not engaging with unknown senders, raising security settings, and reporting to the local FBI field office, 1-800-CALL-FBI, or fbi.gov/tips. — [FBI Threat Intimidation Guide (PDF)](https://www.fbi.gov/file-repository/threat-intimidation-guide-english-022322.pdf)
- Authentication in court. FRE 901(a) asks only for evidence "sufficient to support a finding" that the item is what it's claimed to be. Social media evidence is typically authenticated by:
  - a witness with knowledge, such as the person who took the screenshot (901(b)(1))
  - distinctive characteristics such as handle, profile content, and contextual details (901(b)(4))
  Courts differ in strictness:
  - Maryland's *Griffin* demands more.
  - New York's *Price* says there is no strict formula.
  - The Second Circuit's *Vayner* reversed admission of a profile page that only bore the defendant's name and photo.
  - — [Florida Bar Journal](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/a-prolific-landscape-the-admissibility-of-social-media-postings/); [ABA Business Law Today](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2017-april/authenticating-digital-evidence-at-trial/); [Judge Grimm, Baylor L. Rev.](https://www.txs.uscourts.gov/sites/txs/files/Grimm%20Baylor%20L%20Rev%20Auth%20Digital%20Evid%20pdf%20051117.pdf)
- In *State v. Clemons* (N.C.), screenshots of social-media comments had to be authenticated both as a photograph and as a written statement. The victim's testimony that she took the screenshots satisfied the photo prong. — [UNC SOG](https://nccriminallaw.sog.unc.edu/?p=14053); [NC Courts](https://nccourts.gov/documents/appellate-court-opinions/state-v-clemons-2)

### Inferences
- Practical victim checklist (synthesized from the above):
  1. Capture screenshots or a screen recording showing the comment in context (the parent post, the commenter's handle, the visible timestamp), plus the commenter's profile page. Copy the comment permalink and profile URL as text.
  2. Save a public-archive copy if possible. Note that Instagram is often login-walled and archive services may fail.
  3. Keep a simple log: date and time captured, who captured it, the device, the URL, and a file hash (e.g., SHA-256). Store originals unedited in a read-only folder. This supports 901(b)(1) and (b)(4) authentication later.
  4. Use Instagram's "Download Your Information" to export the account's own data, which includes comments on one's own posts.
  5. Do not delete the comment before capturing it. Hiding, restricting, or blocking is preferable to deletion, and reporting to Instagram after capture is fine.
  6. Ask police to send Meta a preservation request promptly, given the 90-day hold and the fact that data is lost if the user deletes it first.
- Identifying an anonymous commenter generally requires police legal process (a subpoena for subscriber info and IPs). A private civil route exists but requires filing a lawsuit and a court-approved subpoena, and the Stored Communications Act bars providers from disclosing content to private parties.

### Gaps

## 5. Reporting channels and realistic expectations

### Takeaway
- **Imminent danger** (the commenter says he is outside or near the rider now, or a real-world approach has occurred): call 911.
- **Credible but non-imminent threat from an identifiable person:** file a local police report where the victim lives. In LA that means LAPD or the Sheriff, depending on jurisdiction. Bring organized evidence.
- **Federal angle** (interstate, multiple victims, campaign, bias): add an FBI tip at tips.fbi.gov or 1-800-CALL-FBI.
- **IC3 is for internet fraud and cyber-enabled crime, not threats of violence.**
- **Report the comment to Instagram in-app** (violence/threat) after capturing evidence.
- **Meta's law-enforcement portal is police-only.**

### Cited Findings
- IC3 FAQ: "If you or someone else is in immediate danger, please call 911 or your local police." It advises contacting "local law enforcement directly if you believe your matter is time sensitive" and directs terrorism tips to tips.fbi.gov. — [IC3 FAQ](https://www.ic3.gov/Home/FAQ)
- FBI: "If you are in immediate physical danger, call 911. If you experience a threat, please contact your local FBI field office ... or submit a tip via 1-800-CALLFBI ... or via www.fbi.gov/tips. You can also make an anonymous tip." — [FBI Threat Intimidation Guide](https://www.fbi.gov/file-repository/threat-intimidation-guide-english-022322.pdf); [FBI Tips](https://www.fbi.gov/TIPS)
- The FBI tip form covers federal crimes and terrorism, and internet fraud should go to IC3. The FBI's National Threat Operations Center operates 24/7. — [FBI Tips](https://www.fbi.gov/TIPS); [FBI story on internet tip line](https://www.fbi.gov/news/stories/2009/june/tips_062609)
- Meta's Law Enforcement Online Request System requires a government-issued email. It handles preservation requests, emergency disclosure requests (for imminent risk of death or serious physical injury), and legal process. — [Meta LE guidelines](https://www.meta.com/safety/communities/law/guidelines/)
- Guidance for reporting electronic threats advises recording the threat, possibly by screenshot, and including "any associated social media usernames, platforms, or other identifying information." — [Broward College (DOJ-linked guidance)](https://www.broward.edu/safety/_docs/reporting_electronic_threats_to_law_enforcement.pdf)

### Inferences
- Information police want:
  - the exact wording, plus screenshots and URLs
  - handle, display name, profile URL, and any known real name or location
  - date and time
  - why the victim believes it is credible (specificity, local knowledge, repeat contact, a prior road incident)
  - how it affected the victim (fear, changed behavior; relevant to PC 422 "sustained fear")
  - any related prior incidents or reports
- Realistic expectations:
  - Many single anonymous comments produce a report number and no investigation.
  - Action is likelier when the commenter is identifiable and local, threats repeat or escalate, or real-world contact occurred.
  - Even if no charge results, a report number creates a record that supports a later restraining order, platform escalation, or prosecution if things escalate.
  - (Inference; no data source on clearance rates for online threat reports was found.)

### Gaps
- No LAPD- or LASD-specific policy on online threat reports was retrieved. Whether LAPD's online reporting system accepts "criminal threats" (versus requiring an in-person or phone report) is unverified.
- No statistics on how often police or the FBI act on social-media threat reports were found.

## 6. Social media comments as evidence of intent after a road incident

### Takeaway
If a driver who hits a cyclist has posted anti-cyclist threats, those posts may be offered to prove motive or intent, which can turn a negligence case into assault with a deadly weapon (vehicle) or support punitive damages. Admissibility is governed by authentication (FRE 901 / Cal. Evid. Code 1400 et seq.), relevance, and character-evidence rules (FRE 404(b) / Cal. Evid. Code 1101(b) allow other-acts evidence for motive and intent but not propensity). Courts also weigh unfair prejudice (FRE 403 / Evid. Code 352).

### Cited Findings
- FRE 404(b): evidence of other acts is not admissible to prove character or propensity, but "may be admissible for another purpose, such as proving motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake, or lack of accident." [not fetched this session] — [Cornell LII, FRE 404](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_404)
- Authentication requirements for social media posts are covered in Section 4. — [Florida Bar Journal](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/a-prolific-landscape-the-admissibility-of-social-media-postings/)
- Illustrations from news coverage:
  - UK driver Emma Way was identified through her Facebook page after tweeting that she had knocked a cyclist off his bike. — [The World/PRI](https://theworld.org/stories/2016/07/30/uk-driver-emma-way-apparently-knocks-down-cyclist-jokingly-tweets-offense)
  - In a California assault case, defense counsel used the cyclist's own Facebook anti-car posts against him, so posts cut both ways. — [Streetsblog Cal](https://cal.streetsblog.org/2016/05/06/one-year-later-assaulted-cyclist-reflects)
  - In a DC-area case, coverage noted that proving intent is the hard element for prosecutors. — [Washingtonian](https://www.washingtonian.com/2013/04/03/dc-should-be-tougher-on-drivers-who-hit-bikers)

### Inferences
- A preserved, authenticated log of a specific driver's prior threats is most useful if it can be tied to the same person as the driver, for example through the account's real name, vehicle photos, or location. That is why a private, well-documented evidence log is valuable.
- Generic anti-cyclist posts by a driver are weaker 404(b) evidence than a specific threat against the actual victim, and may be excluded under 403/352 as more prejudicial than probative.

### Gaps
- No US appellate case was found that specifically admitted a driver's prior anti-cyclist social media posts to prove intent in a vehicular assault prosecution. The sources found are UK news or anecdotal.

## 7. Risks of compiling or publishing a "database" of commenters

### Takeaway
A **private evidence log** kept for counsel, police, or platform reports carries low legal risk. A **public list** that names commenters as "threateners" or criminals raises real exposure:
- defamation per se if anyone is misidentified or a comment is mischaracterized
- false light
- California's anti-doxing criminal and civil laws if addresses, employers, or other identifying info are published with intent to cause fear or harassment
- possible harassment claims if followers pile on

Section 230 protects a *host* for third-party content but does not protect the person who authors the accusations or labels. California's anti-SLAPP statute may help defend truthful commentary on a public issue, but it is not a shield for false statements.

### Cited Findings
- California PC 653.2(a) is a misdemeanor, up to one year in jail and/or a $1,000 fine. It applies to anyone who, with intent to place another in reasonable fear for their safety (or their immediate family's), by means of an electronic communication device and without consent, for the purpose of imminently causing unwanted physical contact, injury, or harassment by a third party, electronically distributes "personal identifying information, including, but not limited to, a digital image," or a harassing message that would likely incite such action. — [california.public.law PC 653.2](https://california.public.law/codes/penal_code_section_653.2)
- California AB 1979 (Doxing Victims Recourse Act) was signed Sept. 2024, effective Jan. 1, 2025. It added Civil Code 1708.89, a private right of action against doxing. The sponsor states damages can include up to $30,000 for pain and suffering plus fees and costs. Verify against the statute text. — [Asm. Ward press release](https://a78.asmdc.org/press-releases/20240926-assemblymember-ward-bill-allow-victims-doxing-receive-restitution-signed); [LegiScan AB1979 chaptered](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/AB1979/2023); [LAist](https://laist.com/news/doxxing-victims-in-california-may-gain-right-to-sue-to-bring-some-power-back)
- Separately, a 2026 California law restricts publishing personal info of immigration-support workers and was reported as likely to face a First Amendment challenge. It is not directly relevant, but shows the trend of new anti-doxing laws. — [Daily Journal](https://dailyjournal.com/article/393912-newsom-signs-immigration-worker-privacy-bill-setting-up-first-amendment-fight)
- Defamation per se typically involves falsely accusing someone of a crime, and per se claims don't require proof of actual damages. — [ShouseLaw defamation (practice guide)](https://www.shouselaw.com/personal-injury/defamation.html)
- California anti-SLAPP (CCP 425.16) allows a special motion to strike claims arising from speech in connection with a public issue. The plaintiff must then show a probability of prevailing. In *Billauer v. Escobar-Eck* (2023), online posts were held protected activity, yet the plaintiff still showed a probability of success on libel per se, so the motion did not dispose of the claim. — [Justia, Billauer v. ### Inferences
- **Truth is a defense, but labels are risky.** Accurately reposting a public comment verbatim ("@user wrote: 'I'll run you over'") is much safer than characterizing it ("@user is a criminal" or "@user committed a felony"). Calling something a "crime" is an assertion of fact that a court may test, and it is defamatory per se if wrong. Misattribution is a major risk: impersonator or parody accounts, a handle reused by someone else, or linking a handle to the wrong real person.
- **Doxing exposure** arises mainly when a list goes beyond the handle and quote to real names, employers, addresses, or license plates. Combined with a large audience and calls to action, this could be framed as intent to cause harassment by third parties (PC 653.2; Civ. Code 1708.89).
- **CCPA/CPRA** almost certainly does not apply to an individual creator. It applies to for-profit "businesses" meeting revenue or data-volume thresholds. A creator's business entity could theoretically approach the thresholds, but a threat log is unlikely to be the trigger. (Thresholds not verified this session.)
- **Section 230** would protect a hosting platform for user-submitted entries, but the compiler is liable for content they author, such as labels, summaries, and identifications. If the compiler solicits and materially contributes to unlawful content, that may also weaken 230 protection for a crowd-sourced site.
- **Safe design spectrum:**
  - *Lowest risk:* a private, access-controlled evidence log (verbatim text, screenshots, URLs, timestamps, hashes) shared only with police, a lawyer, or platform trust and safety.
  - *Middle:* publicly discussing the problem with anonymized or redacted examples.
  - *Highest risk:* a public, searchable list of named individuals labeled as threateners, especially with off-platform identifying info.
- A public list can also undercut prosecutions (defense claims of taint or bias), provoke retaliation, and give the commenter a counter-narrative of being harassed by the creator's audience.
