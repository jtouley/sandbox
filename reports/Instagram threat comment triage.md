# Route threats through the creator's own API

The lawful way to do this is to use the creator's own account. Phil Gaimon, or a tool acting for him, connects his Instagram professional account to the official Instagram API. Webhooks capture every new comment before anyone hides or deletes it, and a one-time backfill pulls the history. A cheap two-stage classifier sorts comments: the free OpenAI Moderation endpoint or Claude Haiku 5.5 screens everything, and a stronger LLM rubric handles only the hostile remainder. A human then decides which few comments meet the legal "true threat" bar and goes to local police. The FBI is a second channel, not the first. Model costs are trivial: a full backfill of several hundred thousand comments costs roughly $100–$450, and ongoing triage costs about a dollar per thousand comments or less. The hard parts are legal and operational. Classifiers cannot tell a joke from a threat reliably. Under *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023), a criminal threat needs a serious expression of intent to commit violence plus at least reckless awareness that it would be taken as one. Police can act on only a small fraction of comments, usually those from an identifiable, local, or repeat sender. The "scrape everyone into a database for future lawsuits" idea from the original thread is the riskiest version of this project. A private, access-controlled evidence log is low risk. A public list naming commenters as threateners or criminals invites defamation-per-se and anti-doxing claims and can undermine the prosecutions it means to help. Meta's own Platform Terms also bar using API data for law-enforcement "surveillance." So the tool has to be built and described as the creator's moderation and safety tool, with any disclosure to police made on his express direction.

## The creator's own API token beats any scraper

Meta shut down the old Instagram Basic Display API on **December 4, 2024** ([Meta developer blog](https://developers.facebook.com/blog/post/2024/09/04/update-on-instagram-basic-display-api); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/12/06/instagram-locks-out-developers-of-third-party-consumer-apps)). Its replacement, the Instagram API, serves only Business and Creator accounts. That fits this case, because the person being threatened owns the account. With the creator's login and the `instagram_business_basic` + `instagram_business_manage_comments` permissions, `GET /{ig-media-id}/comments` returns each comment's ID, text, ISO-8601 timestamp, commenter username and Instagram-scoped ID, `parent_id`, replies and a `hidden` flag. It returns **at most 50 comments per call** and cannot filter by date ([Meta IG Media Comments](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-media/comments); [Meta IG Comment](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-comment)). Since August 2024, reading other commenters' usernames requires the manage-comments permission. The same token can hide, unhide or delete comments, and webhooks on the `comments` and `live_comments` fields push each new comment in real time. Meta recommends webhooks over polling ([Meta Comment Moderation guide](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/comment-moderation)).

The backfill fits within the platform's limits. Listing an account's media is capped at the **10,000 most recent items** ([Meta IG User Media](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-user/media)), so Gaimon's roughly 8,100 posts should all be reachable. Instagram's rate limit is **4,800 calls × daily impressions** per app–user pair over a rolling 24 hours ([Meta rate limiting](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/graph-api/overview/rate-limiting)). The backfill needs roughly 10^4 to 10^5 calls, which is far below that ceiling for an account of this size. That figure is our estimate; his actual impression count was not checked.

The API has documented blind spots, and they matter here. It does not return comments from users the creator has **Restricted**, comments on age-gated media, or live-video comments after the broadcast ends ([Meta IG Comment](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-comment)). Restricted accounts may be the very abusers who matter, so they need to be unrestricted before the backfill or captured by hand. Meta does not document whether comments auto-filtered by Hidden Words or Limits reach the API. A test account posting a filtered keyword will settle it in minutes. Deleted comments are gone. That makes order of operations an evidence rule: connect webhooks first, backfill second, and only then moderate. When you moderate, hide rather than delete, because a hidden comment keeps its record. Instagram's "Download your information" export is no substitute. It is documented to include comments the account holder *made*, it can take up to 30 days, and no source confirms it includes others' comments on the creator's posts ([Instagram Help](https://help.instagram.com/181231772500920); [PIRG](https://pirg.org/resources/how-to-request-and-download-instagram-data/)).

Scraping is legally survivable but worse in every practical way. Under *Van Buren* (2021) and the Ninth Circuit's *hiQ v. LinkedIn* rulings, collecting public, logged-out pages is unlikely to violate the federal anti-hacking law, the CFAA ([Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-783_k53l.pdf); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/blogs/sourcingatmorganlewis/2022/12/linkedin-v-hiq-landmark-data-scraping-suit-provides-guidance-to-data-scrapers-and-web-operators)). *Meta v. Bright Data* (N.D. Cal. 2024) held that Meta's terms do not bind logged-out non-users ([Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2024/01/game-on-bright-data-scores-major-victory-in-web-scraping-dispute-with-meta-guest-blog-post.htm)). Logged-in scraping is different. hiQ lost on breach of contract over automated collection and fake profiles, and accepted a **$500K consent judgment** plus an order to delete its data ([Proskauer](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2022/12/08/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-in-landmark-scraping-case/)). Instagram's Terms bar automated collection without permission ([Instagram Terms](https://help.instagram.com/581066165581870)). In *X Corp v. Bright Data*, trespass-to-chattels theories based on server load survived a motion to dismiss in late 2024 ([Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2025/01/catching-up-on-the-heavyweight-scraping-battle-between-x-and-bright-data-guest-blog-post.htm)). Scraping from the creator's own login would risk getting the very account you are trying to protect banned.

Commercial scrapers are cheap. Apify charges **$1.90–$2.60 per 1,000 comments** ([Apify](https://apify.com/apify/instagram-comment-scraper)), and Bright Data advertises from **$0.75 per 1,000** ([Bright Data](https://brightdata.com/solutions/data-extraction/instagram/comments)). But they see only the logged-out view. One Apify user reported getting 15 of 640 comments ([Apify issue](https://apify.com/apify/instagram-comment-scraper/issues/only-15-of-the-640-c-msFFNfC154z2j70si)). Scraped data also has no hidden flag, no way to moderate, and none of the platform IDs that make a record easy to authenticate. Scraping makes sense only for watching accounts the victim does not control, which is outside this project.

The real constraint is Meta's Platform Terms, not scraping law. **Section 3.a.iii** prohibits processing Platform Data "for law enforcement or national security purposes" as surveillance, with no safety exception. Section 3.c allows sharing data when the user "expressly directs" it, or when the law requires it ([Meta Platform Terms](https://developers.facebook.com/terms/)). A nonprofit could serve many creators as a Tech Provider once App Review grants Advanced Access. However, §5.b.ii.1 limits it to processing each client's data "on behalf of and at the direction of" that client. In our reading, that rules out cross-creator profiles of the "this handle threatened five athletes" kind. The compliant framing is a creator-controlled moderation and safety tool, where the creator decides what gets exported and sent to police. This is our reading of the terms, not legal advice.

## Classifiers flag hostility well but cannot find a "true threat"

No off-the-shelf moderation API distinguishes the category that matters: a specific threat against an identifiable person versus generalized violent rhetoric. **Perspective API is shutting down on December 31, 2026**. It stopped accepting new quota requests in February 2026 and offers no migration support ([Perspective API](https://perspectiveapi.com/)), so it is a non-starter for a project started now. **OpenAI's omni-moderation endpoint is free**. It returns 0–1 scores for `harassment`, `harassment/threatening`, `violence` and related categories. OpenAI tells users to treat those scores as signals, not decisions, and warns that the silently updated `-latest` model may need its thresholds recalibrated ([OpenAI Moderation guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/moderation)). Azure AI Content Safety rates violence on a 0–7 severity scale, with threats at levels 4–5 and stalking or surveillance at 7 ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/content-safety/concepts/harm-categories)). AWS Comprehend has a `VIOLENCE_OR_THREAT` label ([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/comprehend/latest/dg/trust-safety.html)). Hive charges **$0.50 per 1,000** text requests ([Hive pricing](https://thehive.ai/pricing)). None of them identify who is being threatened. Open-weight guard models such as Llama Guard 4 are built for checking LLM prompts and responses, and a 2026 benchmark found the larger ones conservative, missing up to **75% of unsafe content** ([arXiv 2605.28830](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.28830)).

An LLM following a written rubric with schema-constrained JSON output fills that gap. It can return the five-way label (benign, rude, harassment, generalized violent rhetoric, specific threat). It can also return the target, whether the threat is conditional, any hyperbole markers ("lol", emoji), any reference to the creator's routes or schedule, any mention of a vehicle as a weapon, and a one-sentence rationale quoting the key phrase. That rationale is what a reviewer or lawyer needs. The research consistently favors LLMs over lexicon classifiers, though by modest margins. In one study, GPT models reached **0.71–0.73 accuracy versus 0.66** for Perspective ([arXiv 2309.14517](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.14517)). No public benchmark tests Claude specifically on threat detection.

At current Anthropic list prices, Claude Haiku 5.5 is $0.10/$0.50 per million input/output tokens, Sonnet 5.5 is $2/$10, and the Batch API halves both ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); [batch docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/batch-processing)). Assuming about 1,650 input and 150 output tokens per comment, Haiku 5.5 costs about **$0.24 per 1,000 comments**, or about **$0.12 batched**. Sonnet 5.5 costs about **$4.80 per 1,000**, or $2.40 batched, before any thinking tokens. These are our calculations, not vendor quotes. Two operational details matter. First, Claude can end a response with `stop_reason: "refusal"`, and those items must go to human review rather than being dropped. Second, Anthropic's usage policy bars *generating* violent content inspired by real acts, not *classifying* it ([Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup)).

False positives are the central risk. Lexicon-driven classifiers fire on profanity, emotional language and quoted abuse. A study of a New Zealand tweet bot found **40% false positives** among sampled flagged tweets ([arXiv 2110.00116](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.00116)). Sap et al. found African American English up to **2× more likely** to be labeled offensive ([ACL 2019](https://aclanthology.org/P19-1163/)). Errors run in both directions: false negatives cluster in "indirect, rhetorical, or context-dependent" threats ([arXiv 2603.18015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.18015)), which is exactly how road-rage threats get phrased ("next time I'll just keep driving"). Threat-labeled training data is scarce. The best public sets are the YouTube THREAT corpus ([OsloMet](https://oda.oslomet.no/oda-xmlui/handle/10642/8506)) and InViS ([arXiv 2506.03312](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.03312)). No benchmark covers hyperbolic statements like "I'd run him over" or language aimed at cyclists. Calibration therefore has to happen locally. Hand-label 200–500 of Gaimon's own comments, set the prefilter cutoff for very high recall, define cycling slang ("roll coal", "buzz", "brake check") in the rubric, and keep joke markers as separate fields rather than letting them suppress the label. Context helps judgment ([Risch & Krestel](https://hpi.de/oldsite/fileadmin/user_upload/fachgebiete/naumann/publications/2019/risch2019toxic.pdf)), so give the model the post caption, the parent comment and a short history of the commenter's prior comments on the account. Run a separate gazetteer of the creator's known roads, towns and ride times, so that any mention of them escalates automatically. Keep the classifier as a way to sort the queue. It should never be what decides a police report.

## Few comments clear the legal bar for a police report

Most hostile comments are protected speech or are not worth prosecuting. A true threat is a "serious expression" of intent to commit unlawful violence against a particular person or group. *Counterman v. Colorado*, 600 U.S. 66 (2023), adds that the speaker must at least have "consciously disregard[ed] a substantial risk that his communications would be viewed as threatening violence." Jests and hyperbole are excluded ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-138)). In *Watts* (1969), a conditional remark at a rally that drew laughter was ruled political hyperbole ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/394/705)). In *Elonis* (2015), negligence was held insufficient under the federal threat statute ([Columbia GFoE](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/cases/elonis-v-united-states/)).

Federally, **18 U.S.C. 875(c)** punishes interstate threats to injure with up to five years in prison ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/875)). **18 U.S.C. 2261A(2)** covers cyberstalking, but it requires a course of conduct ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2261A)). Recent DOJ social-media cases, such as an Instagram video calling for judges and police to be killed ([DOJ N.D. Ill.](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ndil/pr/suburban-chicago-man-sentenced-more-three-years-federal-prison-threatening-kill)), skew toward threats against officials, mass-violence threats and bias-motivated threats. That suggests a single comment aimed at a cyclist will usually be a state matter.

In California, the main tool is **Penal Code 422**. It requires a threat of death or great bodily injury that is "unequivocal, unconditional, immediate, and specific" under the circumstances and that causes the victim actual, reasonable and **sustained fear** ([PC 422](https://california.public.law/codes/penal_code_section_422); [ShouseLaw](https://www.shouselaw.com/ca/defense/penal-code/422/)). PC 653m covers threatening electronic contact made with intent to harass ([PC 653m](https://california.public.law/codes/penal_code_section_653m)). A civil harassment restraining order under CCP 527.6 requires clear and convincing evidence. It normally requires more than one act, unless there is a credible threat of violence ([CCP 527.6](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_civ_proc_code_section_527.6)), and the commenter has to be identified and served. Protected speech cannot count toward the course of conduct ([Hansen v. Volkov](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2023/b311524.html)).

Those rules support a decision ladder. The tiers are our synthesis of the factors above, not an official standard.

| Tier | Typical comment | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Imminent | "I'm behind you on Mulholland right now" / real-world approach | Call 911; ask police for an emergency disclosure request to Meta |
| Reportable | Direct "I'll run you over and kill you," especially naming routes, repeating, or from a real-name local account | Preserve, then local police report where the victim lives; request a Meta preservation letter |
| Federal add-on | Same sender threatening many people, sustained campaign, interstate with no local traction, bias angle | FBI tip at tips.fbi.gov or 1-800-CALL-FBI, in addition to local report |
| Log and moderate | Generic "someone should hit these cyclists," conditional or joking violence | Preserve in private log, hide, report to Instagram; watch for escalation from same account |
| Moderate only | Insults, rudeness | Hide or ignore |

What moves a comment up the ladder is specificity of time, place or method, knowledge of the victim's routes or home, repetition or follow-up DMs, a real-name or apparently local account, and references to past incidents. What moves it down is conditional phrasing, obvious exaggeration, a generic class of targets, and a foreign sender. The FBI's guidance is to call 911 for immediate danger and otherwise contact a field office or tips.fbi.gov ([FBI Threat Intimidation Guide](https://www.fbi.gov/file-repository/threat-intimidation-guide-english-022322.pdf); [FBI Tips](https://www.fbi.gov/TIPS)). **IC3 is the wrong channel.** It handles internet fraud and refers people in danger to 911 or local police ([IC3 FAQ](https://www.ic3.gov/Home/FAQ)). A police report should include the exact wording, screenshots, URLs, the handle and profile link, the date and time, why the threat seems credible, and how it changed the victim's behavior. That last point goes directly to PC 422's "sustained fear" element, so describe it honestly, whatever it was. Many single anonymous comments will produce a report number and nothing more. The number still matters: it builds the record that a later restraining order, platform escalation or prosecution can draw on.

## Evidence survives only if it is captured before moderation

Meta will not respond to private parties. It preserves data for **90 days** only on a valid law-enforcement preservation request, and it keeps nothing for law-enforcement purposes if the user deletes the content before such a request arrives ([Meta LE guidelines](https://www.meta.com/safety/communities/law/guidelines/)). Identifying an anonymous commenter requires police legal process. A subpoena gets subscriber records and login IPs, a 2703(d) order gets other non-content records, and a warrant gets content. The pipeline's own record is therefore the first line of preservation. For each flagged comment it should store the raw API JSON, Meta's comment and media IDs, the commenter's username and ID, the timestamp, the post permalink, a full-context screenshot of the comment and of the commenter's profile, a SHA-256 hash, the capture time and the capturing person. Along with each label it should store the classifier model version, prompt version and raw scores. That model-version record matters most for OpenAI's silently upgraded endpoint.

Courts authenticate social-media evidence through a witness with knowledge and through distinctive characteristics under FRE 901. How strict they are varies. *Vayner* (2d Cir.) rejected a profile page that bore only a name and a photo ([Florida Bar Journal](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/a-prolific-landscape-the-admissibility-of-social-media-postings/)). In *State v. Clemons*, screenshots of comments had to be authenticated both as photographs and as statements ([UNC SOG](https://nccriminallaw.sog.unc.edu/?p=14053)). So a person, ideally the creator or a named assistant, should take and be able to vouch for the screenshots. The API record backs them up. We believe that IDs and timestamps from Meta are easier to defend than scraped HTML, though that inference was not tested against evidence-law sources.

A log tied to an identity also has value later. If a driver who strikes a cyclist turns out to have posted threats, FRE 404(b) and Cal. Evid. Code 1101(b) allow other-acts evidence to show motive or intent ([Cornell LII FRE 404](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_404)). That evidence is only as strong as the link between the account and the driver, and it can be excluded as unduly prejudicial. Posts can also cut both ways. In one California assault case, the defense used the cyclist's own anti-car posts against him ([Streetsblog Cal](https://cal.streetsblog.org/2016/05/06/one-year-later-assaulted-cyclist-reflects)).

## A private evidence log is safe; a public "database" is not

The thread's proposal to scrape commenters into a database for future lawsuits combines the two worst design choices. One is broad collection with no triage. The other is a list built to be used against named people. The legal risk depends on what gets published and how it is labeled. Reposting a comment verbatim is defensible. Labeling a person a "criminal" or saying they "committed a felony" is a factual accusation, and if it is wrong it is **defamation per se**, which requires no proof of damages ([ShouseLaw](https://www.shouselaw.com/personal-injury/defamation.html)). Misattribution, such as an impersonator account, a reused handle or a wrong guess at a real name, is the most likely way an accusation turns out false. California's anti-SLAPP statute protects speech on public issues, but in *Billauer v. Escobar-Eck* (2023) online posts that qualified for that protection still faced a libel-per-se claim likely to succeed ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2023/d079835.html)). Section 230 protects hosts of third-party content, not the person who writes the labels.

Adding real names, employers, addresses or license plates creates doxing exposure. California **PC 653.2** criminalizes distributing someone's identifying information electronically with intent to cause fear and to prompt harassment by third parties ([PC 653.2](https://california.public.law/codes/penal_code_section_653.2)). **AB 1979**, effective January 2025, created a civil cause of action for doxing. According to its sponsor, damages include up to $30,000 for pain and suffering ([Asm. Ward](https://a78.asmdc.org/press-releases/20240926-assemblymember-ward-bill-allow-victims-doxing-receive-restitution-signed); [LegiScan](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/AB1979/2023)). The statute text was not read directly, so treat the figure as the sponsor's description. A public list can also feed a defense claim of taint or vigilantism and hand a harasser a story about being victimized. A multi-creator version of the list would breach Meta's Tech Provider terms.

The safe design is narrow. Keep an access-controlled log of flagged comments only. Share it with police, counsel or Instagram's trust and safety team on the creator's direction. Delete comments that were never flagged on a fixed schedule, as Platform Terms §3.d requires ([Meta Platform Terms](https://developers.facebook.com/terms/)). Retain flagged items under a documented evidence-preservation period.

## Recommended architecture costs under $100 a month

The pipeline should run in this order. First, the creator authorizes a small app through Instagram Login. A single-creator deployment needs only Standard Access. Webhooks then write every new comment to an append-only store before the creator moderates anything. Second, a one-time backfill walks all of the creator's media and pages through comments and replies at 50 per call, after any Restricted users are unrestricted and Hidden Words behavior has been tested. Third, every comment goes through a free OpenAI Moderation pass, a Haiku 5.5 rubric call, or both. Fourth, anything rated "harassment or worse" goes to a stronger model, such as Sonnet 5.5, along with the caption, the parent comment, the commenter's history and any location matches. That model returns structured JSON with a quoted rationale. Fifth, a human review queue, sorted by tier and by a per-account escalation score, receives every "specific threat," every low-confidence item about violent rhetoric, every refusal and every location or schedule match. Sixth, approving an item freezes an evidence packet: the API JSON, screenshots, hashes and the capture log. Only then does the tool hide the comment through the API, and it hides rather than deletes. The creator chooses which packets to export into a police report, an FBI tip or an Instagram report.

| Component | Rough cost |
|---|---|
| Instagram API + webhooks | Free (rate limits ample for this account) |
| OpenAI Moderation prefilter | Free |
| Backfill classification, e.g. 400K–1.2M comments (unverified volume) on Haiku 5.5 batch at ~$0.12/1K, plus Sonnet on the ~5–10% flagged | ≈ $100–$450 one time |
| Ongoing triage (thousands of comments/month) | ≈ $1 per 1,000 comments or less; single-digit dollars per month |
| Small server, database, object storage for screenshots | Tens of dollars per month |
| Human review | The real cost: minutes per flagged item, plus initial labeling of 200–500 comments for calibration |

The comment volume assumes 50–150 comments per post across 8,100 posts. That assumption has not been verified, and the flagged share is our estimate. For comparison, paying a vendor to scrape the same volume would cost about $300–$3,000, give incomplete coverage, and provide no way to moderate.

Several unknowns should be resolved in a pilot before relying on the pipeline. These include whether Hidden Words-filtered comments reach the API, how far back comment history goes, Claude's refusal rate when classifying violent text, and whether the local police agency accepts online reports of criminal threats. The notes contained no data on how often police or the FBI act on social-media threat reports.

## Conclusion

Victim safety and evidence do not need a mass surveillance system. They need the opposite: complete capture of one account's comments, done through the channel the account owner controls, followed by aggressive narrowing. AI handles the volume and the sorting, and the stakes justify a decision only after a person has looked. The economics show this clearly. Model costs have fallen so far that classification is effectively free, so the scarce resources are reviewer attention and police attention. The design should spend both on the handful of specific, located or repeated threats and not on the long tail of road-rage venting.

The most important design decision is what *not* to build. A public database of commenters would cost the creator legal protection, platform access and credibility with prosecutors all at once. It would also accomplish nothing that a private, hashed, well-documented evidence log and a police report number do not already accomplish better. If the cycling community wants a shared resource, it should share the open-source tool and the rubric, never a list of names.
